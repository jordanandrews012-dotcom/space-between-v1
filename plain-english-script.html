// Section 1 — The 60-Second Version

If Someone Asks
"What Did You Do?".

This is the short version for a civilian — someone who doesn't work in healthcare or security. Reading time: about 45 seconds. Perfect for coffee chat, LinkedIn DM reply, text message, or explaining to family what you've been up to this week.

The 60-Second Version

I'm a Protection Services Officer at a hospital in Ottawa. A few weeks ago, a woman in acute mental health crisis arrived at our ED with a knife in her vehicle. We waited ninety minutes for police. She ended up arrested instead of treated. I went home angry and started writing.

Three papers later, I've got a policy series arguing that Ontario should designate trained hospital security officers as "Healthcare Special Constables." It's the same legal framework OC Transpo already uses for its transit constables here in Ottawa — it just hasn't been applied to hospitals. Alberta has been doing this for over a decade under a different name and it works.

Why it matters: Canadian hospitals have had a shotgun incident in Manitoba, a shooting in London, stabbings in Halifax, a nurse strangled in Vancouver, sexual assaults at Winnipeg General, a sawed-off in Saskatoon, and a stabbing in Edmonton in the last sixteen months. A mass-casualty event in a Canadian hospital is no longer hypothetical. Right now, no one on-site has the legal authority to act before it becomes a crime.

The series has been submitted to my councillor, my MPP, the Solicitor General, the Premier's office, the Prime Minister's office, and the president of the Ontario Special Constable Association. Now we see what happens.

// Section 2 — Each Paper in Plain English

Three Papers,
Three Paragraphs.

One plain-English paragraph per paper. Drop them in a LinkedIn post, a Reddit comment, a group chat, or an email to anyone who asks "what's in them?" Each one stands alone.

Paper I — Hold the Line (the editorial)

Paper I — Hold the Line. This is the public-facing editorial. It's the "wake-up call" paper. It walks through the Canadian hospital violence record of the last sixteen months — a shotgun in a Manitoba hospital chapel on Christmas Eve 2024, a shooting in a London ER, two nurses stabbed at the Halifax Infirmary, a nurse strangled at Vancouver General, a sexual assault spree at Winnipeg's Health Sciences Centre, a sawed-off shotgun and three knives in a Saskatoon emergency department, a stabbing in Edmonton two weeks ago — and argues that a mass-casualty event in a Canadian hospital is not a matter of if, but when and where. The paper shows that hospitals have every common feature of soft-target mass-casualty environments: open public access, predictable high-density areas, concentrated professional targets, documented weapons flow, and a patient population arriving in first-contact psychiatric crisis at a rate of 45.4%. Nineteen pages, written at the register of a Globe and Mail op-ed.

Paper II — Protecting the Protectors (the frontline case)

Paper II — Protecting the Protectors. This is the frontline case. It makes the argument for why the workforce currently managing these situations — Protection Services Officers in hospitals — deserves and requires legislative recognition under a specialized designation. The paper covers the identity problem ("we are not security guards" — the Ontario regulatory category doesn't match the actual work), the evidence base (98% verbal abuse and 73% physical assault rates for ED security officers in peer-reviewed research; 170 accepted BC hospital security injury claims in a single year), the retention problem (the best officers leave for police services because the current framework doesn't match their skills), the fiscal case (anchored to the Financial Accountability Office's documented $3.4B/$6.4B/$9.6B Ontario health shortfalls), the Alberta proof-of-concept, the training framework, and a direct address to the workforce the paper is written about and from. Twenty-seven pages.

Paper III — The Space Between (the full policy specification)

Paper III — The Space Between. This is the full policy specification. It's the document a legislative aide or a ministry staffer would pick up and say "OK, this is actually a proposal we can work with." Fifty-four pages. It contains: the three-stage failure model (pre-triage, post-triage pre-assessment, escalation during care); a complete legislative authority map; Charter analysis under sections 7, 9, and 15; a clinical primacy enforcement framework with eight specific operational rules (so the officer never overrides clinical judgment); the full training architecture mapped to Ontario Police College standards; an Ottawa two-site pilot design at QCH and The Ottawa Hospital with binding evaluation criteria; a pilot containment framework with automatic off-ramp triggers if it doesn't work; the full fiscal analysis anchored to police-time reallocation as the primary offset; and forty-two references from peer-reviewed, government, and institutional sources. It is the blueprint.

// THE OPENING — THE HOOK

Yeah so you remember that call a few weeks back — the one with the woman in acute mental health crisis, had a knife disclosed in her vehicle, we waited ninety minutes for cops to show up. Headbutted the officer, got arrested, left in handcuffs instead of seeing a doc. That one fucked me up. Went home that night, couldn't sleep, ended up typing. Fifty hours later I've got three papers written on it.

// THE PROBLEM — PLAIN TERMS

Here's the thing nobody says out loud. When someone rolls up to the hospital in a mental health crisis, and they haven't committed a crime yet, nobody on site has the legal authority to touch it. Cops have Section 17 — they can grab someone and bring them in for psych assessment. We can't. Nurses can't. Docs can't do anything until the patient actually hits triage. So for that first thirty minutes, hour, hour and a half — whatever it takes for cops to show — we're literally just standing there watching it unfold. Because the law says we can act after a crime, not before. That space between someone showing up and someone getting care? That's where people fall through. And we all know it because we've all stood in it.

// THE FIX — ALBERTA AND WHAT WE ALREADY HAVE

Alberta figured this out like a decade ago. Their hospital security guys are "Community Peace Officers" under the Alberta Peace Officer Act. They can actually deal with mental health presentations on hospital property. Officer handles it, gets the patient in front of a doc, job done. No ninety-minute wait. No headbutting.

And here's the kicker — Ontario already has the exact same mechanism. It's called a Special Constable designation under the Community Safety and Policing Act. OC Transpo guys work under it right here in Ottawa. The legal framework already exists. It's just never been applied to hospitals. That's the whole fight. Take a thing that already works for transit, apply it to us.

// THE THREE PAPERS — ONE LINE EACH

Paper One — Hold the Line. That's the loud one. Makes the case that with everything going on — Manitoba chapel shotgun, London ER shooting, Halifax stabbings, Vancouver nurse strangled, Winnipeg HSC sexual assaults, Saskatoon St. Paul's shotgun, Edmonton Royal Alex stabbing two weeks ago — a mass-casualty in a Canadian hospital is not a "will it happen" question anymore. It's when and where. And we're the guys at the door when it does.

Paper Two — Protecting the Protectors. This is us. Who we actually are, what we actually do, why the best guys keep leaving for police services, what the training would look like, how it'd get paid for, what it'd look like in practice. Basically: we're already doing the job. The law just hasn't caught up.

Paper Three — The Space Between. Fifty-four pages, the big one. The full policy spec. Every detail. The kind of thing a lawyer or a civil servant picks up and actually takes seriously. Not a rant — a roadmap.

And to Sarah Kennedy, the president of the Ontario Special Constable Association, who's actually a ten-year transit Special Constable here in Ottawa and sits on the government committee that's working on the CSPA regs right now. // THE ASK

Why am I telling you this? Two reasons. One — if anyone asks you about it, you can point them to it. I've got all three papers online, free to read, free to share. I'll get you the link. Two — and this is the big one — this kind of change doesn't happen because a politician decides to be nice to us. It happens because the workforce makes the case. Nurses did it. Paramedics did it. Corrections did it. Every time, it was the people doing the job who forced the legislation to catch up. Now it's our turn.

You don't have to do anything. Just know it exists. Read it if you want. Tell the guys you trust. When it comes up — and if we get enough visibility it will — you can say "yeah I know that guy, read his papers, the thing checks out."

// Section 6 — FAQ for When People Push Back

If Someone Says….

Predictable pushback lines and how to answer them. Stay calm. The papers already contain the detailed answer — you're just giving them the headline and pointing to the source.

// If someone says

"Why don't you just become a cop?"

"Because we're already doing the hospital job — and it's a different job than policing. Hospitals need a designation built for the clinical environment. Alberta figured that out. Ontario hasn't yet. The policing pathway exists, but it doesn't fix the workforce gap in the hospital."

// If someone says

"This sounds expensive."

"It's actually a reallocation proposal, not new spending. The officers are already deployed. The police time currently absorbed by hospital calls gets reclaimed. The Ottawa Police Service's own data shows 250 officer-hours per platoon freed up when Special Constables replace police at lower-priority calls. FAO says the province is $9.6 billion short by 2027-28 — this proposal helps, it doesn't hurt."

// If someone says

"Isn't this just giving security guards police powers?"

"No. The designation has defined limits. It's bounded by hospital property. It's mental health apprehension authority specifically — not criminal investigation, not patrol, not firearms. And the clinical primacy framework built into the proposal means the physician still makes the clinical call. The officer bridges to care; the officer does not become a cop."

// If someone says

"What about Charter rights?"

"Paper Three does full Charter analysis under sections 7, 9, and 15. The framework is built with oversight, training, body-worn camera requirements, and complaint mechanisms matching the existing Special Constable framework OC Transpo already operates under. It's not novel — it's an application of an existing structure."

// If someone says

"Who are you to write this?"

"A Protection Services Officer who's been in this work for a decade, who stood in the gap on the night that prompted it, and who wrote the papers because nobody else was. The policy doesn't need me to be a lawyer or a professor — it needs someone who knows what the gap actually looks like. I'm not claiming to be the one who writes the final legislation. I'm claiming to be the one making the case for why it needs writing."

// If someone says

"What does QCH think of this?"

"The series is a personal submission. I'm not writing for QCH, and every paper's footer makes that explicit. My union is aware. Nothing in the series uses proprietary hospital information or references any specific patient."
