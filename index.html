Why This Exists

A Policy Series
From the Floor.

On a shift in April 2026, a woman in acute psychiatric crisis arrived at our emergency department. A knife had been disclosed in her vehicle. Police took approximately ninety minutes to respond. Section 17 of the Ontario Mental Health Act was never invoked. She left the hospital in handcuffs rather than in clinical care.

That incident is not an outlier. It is a specific instance of a structural gap in Ontario's legislative framework — a gap in which no professional continuously present on hospital property holds the legal authority to bridge a person in crisis to clinical assessment before a crime has been committed. In the last sixteen months, that gap has intersected with a documented and accelerating pattern of violence inside Canadian hospitals: a shotgun in a Manitoba hospital chapel, a shooting in a London emergency room, stabbings at the Halifax Infirmary, a nurse strangled at Vancouver General, sexual assaults at Winnipeg's Health Sciences Centre, a sawed-off shotgun in Saskatoon's St. Paul's, a stabbing at Edmonton's Royal Alexandra two weeks ago.

The three papers below are the case for closing that gap. They propose the designation of trained hospital Protection Services Officers as Healthcare Special Constables under Ontario's Community Safety and Policing Act, Part XIV — the same legal mechanism under which Ottawa's OC Transpo Special Constable Unit already operates, and an adaptation of a model Alberta has run within its provincial health system for over a decade.
